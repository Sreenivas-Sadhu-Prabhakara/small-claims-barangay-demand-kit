    How it works

    Two gates stand between an unpaid invoice and a judgment, and most do-it-yourself filings fall at the first.

    Gate one is the barangay. Under the Katarungang Pambarangay Law — Sections 399 to 422 of the Local Government Code of 1991 — the lupon of each barangay has authority over disputes between parties actually residing in the same city or municipality. For any dispute within that authority, Section 412(a) makes confrontation before the lupon chairman or the pangkat a pre-condition to filing anywhere: no complaint may be instituted directly in court unless conciliation was tried and failed, as certified by the Lupon or Pangkat Secretary, or the settlement was repudiated. A complaint that needed that referral and does not show compliance is dismissed without prejudice, on the court's own initiative or on the defendant's motion, and may be re-filed only once the requirement is met. So this tool decides the gate before it writes anything, and tells you which of the two documents you are allowed to file next.

    The gate is opened by the enumerated exceptions, which this tool checks one by one against Supreme Court Administrative Circular No. 14-93 — the same twelve-item list a first-level judge checks a complaint against. The one that matters most to a small business: a complaint by or against a corporation, partnership or other juridical entity is excepted, because only individuals may be parties to barangay conciliation. A sole proprietorship is not a juridical entity. A registered business name has no personality separate from its owner, so the owner is the party, and conciliation still applies. That single distinction is the most common reason a collection case gets thrown out.

    Gate two is the ceiling. A money claim within the small claims ceiling goes under Rule IV of the Rules on Expedited Procedures in the First Level Courts, where the hearing is informal, the decision comes within twenty-four hours and is final, and no attorney may appear for either side. The ceiling is measured on the claim exclusive of interest and costs, so interest that has run does not push you out of it. Past the ceiling the case goes to summary procedure, and past the summary ceiling to the regular rules in the Regional Trial Court — where you will want a lawyer.

    Everything else the tool prints follows from those two answers: the demand letter's closing paragraph names the forum you can actually use next, the barangay complaint is laid out for the barangay that has venue, and the Statement of Claim carries the conciliation statement your pleading must contain — either compliance with a certification attached, or the exception that excuses it.

    Assumptions, figures and authorities

    Every figure this tool uses, and where it comes from

    Small claims ceiling — ₱1,000,000.00, measured on the claim exclusive of interest and costs. Rules on Expedited Procedures in the First Level Courts, A.M. No. 08-8-7-SC, Rule IV, Section 1 (see also Rule I A(2) and Rule IV, Sections 6 and 15), effective 11 April 2022. Authority: the Supreme Court of the Philippines. Indicative. This ceiling has been raised repeatedly — ₱100,000 when small claims began, then ₱200,000, ₱300,000 and ₱400,000 before the 2022 rules set the present figure — and it can be raised again by a single en banc resolution without any change in statute. We last checked it on 24 November 2026 against the Supreme Court's published text of A.M. No. 08-8-7-SC; we could not find a later amendment, but absence of a find is not proof of absence. Confirm the current figure with the Office of the Clerk of Court before you file. If it has moved, this page is wrong until the constant is updated.

    Summary procedure ceiling — ₱2,000,000.00, exclusive of interest, damages, attorney's fees, litigation expenses and costs. A.M. No. 08-8-7-SC, Rule I A(1)(b); it tracks the first-level jurisdiction set by Republic Act No. 11576 (2021). Also indicative.

    Barangay periods (Local Government Code of 1991): mediation by the Punong Barangay, up to 15 days from the first meeting, s.410(b); the pangkat convenes within 3 days of constitution and has 15 days, extendible by 15, to settle, s.410(d) and (e); prescription is interrupted while the dispute is before the barangay, by no more than 60 days, s.410(c); a settlement has the force of a final judgment after 10 days, s.416; it may be repudiated within 10 days for vitiated consent, s.418; the lupon may execute it for 6 months, after which enforcement moves to court, s.417.

    Small claims periods and fees (A.M. No. 08-8-7-SC, Rule IV): summons issues within 24 hours of filing and is served within 10 days, ss.10 and 12; the defendant has a non-extendible 10 calendar days to file a verified Response, s.13; the hearing is set within 30 calendar days of filing, or 60 if a defendant is outside the judicial region, s.10; one postponement only, s.20; decision within 24 hours of the hearing, final, executory and unappealable, ss.22 and 24. Docket fees follow Rule 141 of the Revised Rules of Court on the amount claimed, plus ₱1,000.00 for service of summons, which is never waived — not even for a party allowed to sue as an indigent, s.8. A progressive surcharge of ₱500.00, then ₱600.00, then ₱700.00 per claim applies after your fifth, tenth and fifteenth small claim in a calendar year, s.8.

    Prescriptive periods (Civil Code of the Philippines): ten years on a written contract, Art. 1144(1); six years on an oral one, Art. 1145(1). The tool counts from the date you say the debt fell due, which is a simplification — the period runs from when the right of action accrued, and part payments or written acknowledgements can restart it.

    Barangay conciliation exceptions: Local Government Code of 1991, s.408(a) to (g) and s.412(b), as listed by the Supreme Court in Administrative Circular No. 14-93 of 15 July 1993. The juridical-entity exception is item I.4 of that circular, which cites Section 1, Rule VI of the Katarungang Pambarangay Rules.

    The demand period of 15 days is this tool's own suggestion, not a statutory period. Nothing in law fixes it; pick what suits the relationship.

    Not modelled: criminal cases of any kind, including estafa and bouncing cheques under Batas Pambansa Blg. 22; ejectment and unlawful detainer; the enforcement of a court judgment; venue rules for lenders and banks beyond the warning the tool shows; and the special rules for indigenous cultural communities beyond flagging that they apply. The tool also takes your answers at face value: it cannot verify where anybody actually resides, and the legal test is actual residence, not a billing address.

    Primary sources: the Supreme Court's publication of A.M. No. 08-8-7-SC, and the official small claims forms at the Office of the Court Administrator.

    This is not legal advice. It is a reading of published rules applied to facts you typed in, and it can be wrong — about your facts, about your barangay's practice, or because the rules changed after this page was written. Procedure and ceilings change: the small claims ceiling has been raised four times, and a Supreme Court resolution can move it again overnight. Nothing here creates a lawyer-client relationship. For anything substantial, or any claim close to a prescriptive deadline, consult a lawyer, the Public Attorney's Office, or the Office of the Clerk of Court, all of which will help you for free or for very little.
